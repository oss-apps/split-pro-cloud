Group trips are the best and the most expensive way to find out how your friends think about money. One person books the house, another fills up the car, a third keeps buying rounds, and by the last night nobody is sure who is ahead.

It doesn't have to be stressful. Groups that come back from trips still friends usually do three things: they agree on a few rules before leaving, they write things down as they go, and they settle up quickly afterwards.

## Before you go: agree on four things

A five-minute conversation in the group chat saves hours later.

**1. What is shared, and what isn't.** Accommodation, car rental, fuel and groceries are almost always shared. Souvenirs, spa treatments and that bottle of wine only one person drank usually aren't. Decide on the grey areas, such as group dinners or activities not everyone joins, before they happen.

**2. Who books the big things.** The house and the flights are often paid months in advance by one person. Make sure everyone knows those count, even if they were paid long before the trip.

**3. Which currency you'll settle in.** If you're traveling abroad, record each expense in the currency it was paid in, and agree to settle at the end in one currency everyone uses.

**4. When you'll settle.** "Within a week of getting home" is a good default. Debts that linger are the ones that cause trouble.

## During the trip: write it down when it happens

The single best habit is adding each expense **when it happens**, not that evening and definitely not the week after. You remember who was at that lunch right now. In three days you won't.

A few things that help:

- **One person per payment.** Whoever pays adds it. That way nothing is added twice, and nothing is forgotten.
- **Snap the receipt.** A photo settles most arguments before they start.
- **Mark who was in.** If only four of six went kayaking, split it between those four. It feels fussy, but it is what keeps a split fair when people do different things.
- **Don't round in your head.** Enter the real amount. Small roundings add up over a week.

## After the trip: settle in as few payments as possible

At the end, the naive approach is for everyone to pay back everyone they owe. With six people that can mean ten or more small transfers.

There's a better way. Work out each person's **balance**: what they paid minus their share of everything. Then match the people who owe money with the people who are owed. Here's a real example from a weekend at a cabin:

|                         | Paid    | Share   | Balance  |
| ----------------------- | ------- | ------- | -------- |
| Alex (booked the cabin) | $480.00 | $222.10 | +$257.90 |
| Priya (groceries)       | $126.40 | $198.10 | −$71.70  |
| Jordan (gas)            | $72.00  | $222.10 | −$150.10 |
| Sam (Saturday dinner)   | $186.00 | $222.10 | −$36.10  |

Priya didn't come along for the drive, so her share of the gas is zero. Paying back each expense directly would take 11 transfers. Using balances, it takes three:

- Jordan pays Alex **$150.10**
- Priya pays Alex **$71.70**
- Sam pays Alex **$36.10**

## A few tricky situations

**Someone paid a deposit months ago.** Add it to the trip like any other expense. The date doesn't matter, only who paid and who it was for.

**One person left early.** Leave them out of the expenses after they left. Their share of the shared house usually still counts, unless you agreed otherwise.

**Someone wants to "just cover it".** Generosity is lovely, but it makes the next trip awkward for whoever can't do the same. Record it anyway, and let the person who paid decide later whether to forgive it.

**Different budgets.** If one friend is on a tighter budget, agree up front which costs are optional. A clear "I'll skip the fancy dinner" is much easier than a quiet resentment.

## The short version

1. Agree on what's shared, the currency, and when you'll settle.
2. Add every expense when it happens, with who was in.
3. Settle within a week, using balances, in as few payments as possible.

Have a good trip.
